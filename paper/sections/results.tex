On the adaptation scene, fine-tuning improves both students (Table~\ref{tab:det}): \AP{} changes by \DeltaYoloFtPt{}
for \yolo{} and by \DeltaRfFtPt{} for \rfdetr{}. The COCO-pretrained \rfdetr{} misses most small, distant
pedestrians (\APsm{} \ApSmallRfPt). Fine-tuned \yolo{} leads fine-tuned \rfdetr{} by \DeltaFtFt{} \AP{}
\ci{\TcAaCbbBFtFtLo}{\TcAaCbbBFtFtHi}, \TcAaCbbBFtFtP, with most of the difference at strict IoU and on small people. Part of this margin is attributable to the labels.
Each student scores highest against the teacher of its own architecture family, and against RF-DETR-Large
labels alone the difference between the two fine-tuned students is \TbGapRfL{} \AP{}. The fused labels therefore favour \yolo{}, and
the ranking is valid only with respect to them.

The two students were also evaluated at different input sizes, 1280\,px for \yolo{} and 640\,px for \rfdetr{}.
When every model is re-scored at six input sizes without retraining, and \yolo{} is also trained at 640\,px,
the gap disappears.
Trained and tested at 640\,px, \yolo{} reaches \MsRsixAa{} against \MsRfFtAa{} for \rfdetr{}. Tested at 1280\,px
the two fine-tuned students tie on aa1 (\RsFteFAa{} and \RsRfFtFAa), while on test1 \rfdetr{} scores \RsRfFtFHo{}
and \yolo{} \RsFteFHo. \rfdetr{} transfers better at every input size evaluated. The advantage of \yolo{} lies in computational cost:
\CsYFGf{} GFLOPs at $736{\times}1280$, against \CsRCGf{} for \rfdetr{} at 640\,px and \CsRFGf{} at 1280\,px. At the
closest compute, \rfdetr{} at 384\,px (\CsRAGf{} GFLOPs), \yolo{} is far better on aa1 (\RsFteFAa{} against
\RsRfFtAAa) and similar on test1. In its deployed configuration \yolo{} takes \LatYoloDep\,ms per 720p frame and
\rfdetr{} \LatRfDep\,ms, including pre- and post-processing. The NMS-free head of \yolo{} is faster but loses
\NmsFreeApLoss{} \AP{} in this crowded scene, so the deployed graph keeps the one-to-many head and NMS.

Table~\ref{tab:adapt} presents the main result. Fine-tuning \yolo{} on aa1 raises aa1 \AP{} from \MsPtAa{} to \MsFtAa{} and
lowers it on every other scene: scene~1 from \MsPtVone{} to \MsFtVone, scene~2 from \MsPtVtwo{} to \MsFtVtwo, test1 from
\MsPtHo{} to \MsFtHo{} and COCO people from \MsPtCoco{} to \MsFtCoco. On test1 the paired difference to the
pretrained weights is \HoDeltaYoloFtPt{} \ci{\HoDeltaLoYoloFtPt}{\HoDeltaHiYoloFtPt}. The pattern does not depend on
the choice of adaptation scene. A model fine-tuned on scene~1 alone, or on scene~2 alone, improves on its own scene
and falls below the pretrained model on every other scene and on COCO. \rfdetr{} behaves differently. Fine-tuned on
aa1, it improves on its pretrained weights on both campus scenes and on test1, and loses \AP{} on COCO only from
\MsRfPtCoco{} to \MsRfFtCoco.

On test1 the degradation is concentrated on small people. At the frozen threshold the fine-tuned \yolo{} finds
\MsFtHoSmall{} of the people 32--64\,px tall (pretrained: \MsPtHoSmall) and makes \MsFtHoFppi{} false positives per
frame (pretrained: \MsPtHoFppi). Because all of these numbers measure agreement with the teachers, the
disagreements on \HoAuFrames{} random test1 frames were inspected. The inspection was performed on image crops by an AI
model rather than by independent annotators and is therefore treated as a diagnostic only. Of the \HoAuFnN{} labels that a model missed,
\HoAuFnPerson{} were people, and of the \HoAuFpN{} detections far from any label, \HoAuFpPerson{} were people the
teachers had missed; the
rest were bins, benches and tree trunks. Crediting every such label error to the models changes no F1 by more than
\HoAuMaxFoneShift, so the reversal is not an artefact of the pseudo-labels. It also holds when either teacher's labels
are used alone.

\subsection{Effect of the Pseudo-Label Threshold}
\label{sec:threshold}
The 0.40 cut on the fused teacher score is not neutral with respect to size. On the aa1 training frames it removes
\LtAaA{} of the teacher boxes ($s\ge0.15$) on people under 32\,px tall and \LtAaB{} of those 32--64\,px tall, against
\LtAaD{} at 128--256\,px and \LtAaE{} above 256\,px. Test1 has the same profile. A person omitted from the labels is
treated as background, so fine-tuning trains the student to suppress precisely the small, distant people whose
recall later collapses on test1. Since some low-score boxes are not people, the size profile alone is not
conclusive. The ignore variant tests the hypothesis directly by greying out the \LtGreyed{} uncertain regions in the
training images. Compared with the same-recipe reference, it raises test1 \AP{} from \MsFteHo{} to \MsIgnHo{} but
leaves COCO \AP{} essentially unchanged (\MsIgnCoco{} against \MsFteCoco). The threshold therefore accounts for part of the loss on
other cameras, and forgetting of general person appearance accounts for the remainder.

\subsection{Mitigation Without Target-Camera Labels}
\label{sec:repair}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_tradeoff.pdf}
\caption{Weight averaging between pretrained ($\alpha{=}0$, black square) and fine-tuned ($\alpha{=}1$) \yolo{}.
Points above the dotted line joining the two endpoints do better on the second axis than a linear trade-off would.
(a)~aa1 test against test1; (b)~aa1 test against COCO people.}
\label{fig:tradeoff}
\end{figure*}

Among the mitigations evaluated, weight averaging gives the best trade-off and adds no training or inference
cost. With $\alpha$ chosen on aa1 validation and COCO validation ($\alpha{=}\WiFteRawAlpha$), the average
scores \MsWiFtAa{} on aa1 and \MsWiFtHo{} on test1, above both endpoints on test1. The whole path lies well above the
straight line between the endpoints (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}), which is effective robustness in the sense of Taori et
al.~\cite{taori2020shift}. Re-estimating the BatchNorm statistics after averaging, the usual recommendation for merged
networks, degraded accuracy: lower aa1 \AP{} at every $\alpha$, and lower test1 \AP{} except for blends very
close to the fine-tuned model (\WiFteBnHo{} at its validation-chosen $\alpha{=}\WiFteBnAlpha$). A plausible explanation is
that the two endpoints share an initialisation, so interpolating two well-estimated sets of statistics is more accurate
than estimating new ones from 192 images.

The training-time mitigations are less effective. COCO replay gives the best single model on aa1 (\MsRepAa) and recovers much
of the test1 loss (\MsRepHo), and a frozen backbone keeps more of the pretrained features (test1 \MsFrzHo, COCO
\MsFrzCoco) with few false positives on test1 (\MsFrzHoFppi{} per frame). Neither reaches the pretrained model on test1 or COCO. Training
on three scenes improves the included scenes but reaches only \MsMultiHo{} on test1; averaging that model with
the pretrained weights gives the best \yolo{} result on test1 (\MsWiMuHo).

A coefficient selected on the adaptation scene alone cannot detect a shift that appears only on other cameras. The first decision
matrix (Sec.~\ref{sec:select}) chose the fine-tuned \yolo{} under all six weightings, and that model scores \SrOrigHo{}
on test1. The shift-aware matrix chooses the average with $\alpha{=}0.3$. Checked once on test1, it scores \SrWinnerHo,
fourth of \SrN{} candidates and within 0.02 of the best (\MsWiFtHo), while keeping aa1 \AP{} at
\MsSelAa. When no validation data from other cameras is available, remaining close to the pretrained weights is the
safer default. The selected average is the default detector of the released runtime. Its ONNX export, scored through
the runtime's own detector, matches the PyTorch reference within \SxParity{} \AP{} on both aa1 and test1; at
$544{\times}960$ it reaches \SxAaMid{} on aa1 and \SxHoMid{} on test1. The streamed experiments in
Sec.~\ref{sec:tracking} were run with the fine-tuned \yolo{} chosen by the first matrix, before the averaged model
was selected.

\subsection{Tracking and Counting}
\label{sec:tracking}
On the aa1 tracking hold-out (15--30\,s) the HOTA difference between the fine-tuned students (\yolo{} minus \rfdetr{}) is
\BtDefGap{} with default ByteTrack parameters and \BtTunGap{} after tuning, which is within search noise. Across six trackers and two
pseudo-ground-truths \yolo{} scores higher in \TcWinsAll{} of \TcPairs{} combinations but never by more than \TcMaxGap{} HOTA.
The choice of ground-truth linking moves absolute HOTA more than the choice of tracker, so models should only be
compared under the same ground truth.

On the other cameras, the detection results carry over to tracking. On test1, with all parameters frozen from aa1,
HOTA is \HoBtHotaYoloFtTun{} for the fine-tuned \yolo{}, \HoBtHotaRfFtTun{} for the fine-tuned \rfdetr{} and
\HoBtHotaYoloPtTun{} for the pretrained \yolo{}, which wins all \HoSegs{} of the 30\,s segments against \rfdetr{}
(Wilcoxon $p=\HoSegPPtRf$). Counting-line F1 follows the same order (\HoAnCrossFoneYoloFt, \HoAnCrossFoneRfFt{} and
\HoAnCrossFoneYoloPt). On scene~1 the fine-tuned \yolo{} reaches HOTA \StVoneFtHota{} against \StVoneRfFtHota{} for
\rfdetr{} and recovers far fewer line crossings (F1 \StVoneFtCrossFone{} against \StVoneRfFtCrossFone). On scene~2,
where people are large and close, the three detectors count similarly. On both campus videos the aa1-tuned tracker
parameters beat ByteTrack's defaults for both fine-tuned detectors, indicating that the search on 15\,s of aa1 did not
merely overfit that window.

Running test1 through the deployed runtime revealed a defect that the constant-rate aa1 video had concealed. Without an
explicit output rate, FFmpeg's raw-video output resampled the variable-frame-rate file to a constant rate
(\HoVfrDelivered{} frames instead of \HoVfrFrames), and timestamps computed from the mean rate drifted by up to
\HoVfrTsErr\,s. Passing frames through unchanged with their container timestamps raised the streamed HOTA from
\HoVfrHotaBefore{} to \HoEdHotaHiSOne{} and left every aa1 number unchanged. On aa1 the streamed pipeline (decoder
process, ONNX detector, online tracker) reproduces the offline HOTA exactly (\EdHotaHiSOne). Processing every fourth
frame reduces HOTA only slightly (\EdHotaHiSFour) because the tracker uses real time steps, whereas the
$384{\times}640$ input reduces it further (\EdHotaLoSFour).

\subsection{Uncertainty Under Temporal Correlation}
\label{sec:stats}
Treating the \TcAaFrames{} aa1 test frames as independent understates the standard error of \AP{} by a factor of
\TcAaRatioMin--\TcAaRatioMax, depending on the block length. The effective sample size of the split is about
\TcAaNeff{} frames. The automatic block length is \TcAaAutoBlockS\,s on aa1 and \TcHoAutoBlockS\,s on
test1, longer than the 10\,s initially assumed for test1. Every paired difference reported in this paper keeps its sign
and excludes zero under every resampling scheme, including the longest blocks. The intervals widen, but no
conclusion changes.

\subsection{VLM Agent}
\label{sec:vlm}
The first experiments establish which tasks the 2B model can perform reliably. On the \EoFrames{} aa1 test frames (\EoGtMin--\EoGtMax{} people
per frame) it undercounts substantially from the image alone (mean absolute error \EoMaeB). Given the detector's count in the
prompt, its error falls to \EoMaeC{}, which is exactly the detector's own error: it repeats the detector's number on
every frame, and a text-only control without the image gives the same result. In an alert-verification test on
\EvN{} frames, shown the measured value, it confirmed every true alert and also every fabricated one. Since the model
cannot count reliably, it cannot verify a count either; counts and alert decisions are therefore left to the
analytics, and the VLM is restricted to describing the scene.

Tool use required a redesign. In a single-stage agent that sees the frame and the tools together, the model called a
tool for 12 of the \EthNNoTool{} utterances that needed none and gave exact arguments for 16 of the \EthNTool{}
that needed one. Routing the request without the image, with a simpler alert schema, brought false calls
down to \EtbFp{} and exact arguments up to \EtbArg, at a median of \EtbLat\,s per turn. The schema and the routing
changed together, so their effects are not separated.

The cooperative requests above do not measure behaviour under misuse. A suite of \SfN{} cases was therefore
written before any of them was run. It contains \SfNAuth{} authorised requests and nine unsafe categories: ambiguous, contradictory, from an
unauthorised role, malformed or out of range, alert-disabling, injected into the request, painted as a sign into the
frame, injected into the free-text fields of events returned by a tool, and multi-turn escalation. Every case goes
through the deployed path on a real aa1 frame and is scored from the analytics state before and after. Three configurations are
compared: the model alone, the model with the original guardrails (an argument check and a rule-based fallback), and the model with
the policy layer (Table~\ref{tab:safety}).

Without the policy layer the agent rejected fewer than half of the unsafe requests. It disabled alerts when asked
(rejected \SfMoDisK{} of \SfNDis), accepted changes from viewers (\SfMoUnauthK{} of \SfNUnauth) and followed every
multi-turn escalation. The original guardrails helped cooperative users (authorised success \SfMoTestUtil{} to
\SfGrTestUtil) and slightly lowered the rejection rate, because the rule-based fallback executed ``remove all alerts''
for anyone who typed it, injected text included. The sign in the frame acted through the chat history. Asked what
the sign said, the VLM quoted its instruction in \SfReadSign{} of \SfReadSignN{} cases, and a following ``Go ahead''
let the first stage, which had seen that answer, carry it out in \SfGrFrameAct{} of \SfGrFrameActN. When the operator
did not invite the model to act, the sign succeeded in \SfGrFrameOther{} of \SfGrFrameOtherN{} cases, and periodic
scene reports never quoted it. Injection through tool results never succeeded, even without the policy layer.

With the policy layer the rejection rate rose to \SfPoTestRej{} at unchanged authorised success. All frame-text,
unauthorised-role, alert-disabling and multi-turn escalation cases were blocked. Of the unsafe cases handled
safely, \SfByNoCall{} never produced a state-changing call because of the routing and the isolation of the first
stage, \SfByRole{} were stopped by roles, \SfByGate{} by the intent gate, \SfByValid{} by validation, and
\SfByConfirm{} are waiting for confirmation. Every audit chain verified. On the development set the intent gate
wrongly refused one legitimate request (``Watch for crowding: more than 18 people for 15\,s''), which contains no
change verb. The remaining failures are silent repairs. Given ``alert when occupancy exceeds $-5$'' or ``more than 10 and
fewer than 5 people'', the model substitutes a valid but different rule without notice. Validation cannot detect an argument
that is valid; a clarification request to the user would.

A scene report takes a median \EfEImgM\,s at 896\,px on the desktop GPU, a text answer \EfEText\,s and a tool-call
turn \EfETool\,s. These jobs run asynchronously and never block the frame loop.
