# Literature maps each reviewer demand to experiments

Every one of the reviewer's six methodological demands has a well-precedented answer, and most can be met cheaply on existing hardware. The single most valuable experiment is a **weight-space interpolation sweep between the COCO-pretrained and fine-tuned YOLO26n**. It needs no training, and WiSE-OD (WACV 2026) shows it beating both endpoints in-domain and under shift for four detector families, including YOLOv8n ([WiSE-OD](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18925v2)). Combined with Scenes100-style 1:1 COCO replay and backbone freezing, it turns the 0.684 → 0.412 held-out-camera collapse from an embarrassment into a measured ID-versus-OOD trade-off curve. On statistics, the 182-frame test set holds only 5 to 19 temporal blocks, so the paper must report a paired circular block bootstrap at 10/20/40 frames. It must also restate "p < 0.001" as p = 1/(B+1) under the Phipson–Smyth convention ([Phipson & Smyth](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.05766)), and name the video, not the frame, as the unit of generalisation. For the VLM, AgentDojo's utility / utility-under-attack / targeted-ASR triple ([AgentDojo](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13352v3)), applied to a stratified suite of about 300 cases, supports the claim that safety does not depend on the 2B model. That suite should include typographic injections rendered into real frames, and the claim needs an ablation separating the model alone from the model plus a deterministic policy layer. On Jetson, the published reference numbers (about 4.6 ms for YOLO26n TensorRT FP16 at 640 on Orin Nano Super; about 151 ms ViT + 191 ms prefill + 36.9 tok/s decode for Qwen3-VL-2B on Orin Nano 8GB) predict headroom. Detector-plus-VLM concurrency on 8 GB of shared memory, however, is unmeasured anywhere in the literature, which makes it both mandatory and publishable. The human-annotation and resolution items have equally concrete templates: MOT16/Caltech labelling rules with Krippendorff's α, and a common-resolution sweep that respects RF-DETR Nano's native 384 px.

## Weight interpolation can likely recover most of the held-out-camera loss

The literature reframes the paper's headline failure. Scene-specific self-training on fixed cameras usually yields **modest in-scene gains of +0.8 to +4.5 AP**. Several popular domain-adaptation methods (STAC, Adaptive Teacher, H2FA, TIA) actually lowered AP on the 100-video Scenes100 benchmark, and LODS degraded "severely" with any schedule longer than 1,000 iterations ([Zhang & Hoai, CVPR 2023](https://www3.cs.stonybrook.edu/~minhhoai/papers/Self-Supervised_Scene_Adaptation_CVPR23.pdf)). Against that baseline, a +6.5 AP50:95 in-scene gain paired with a −27 AP held-out loss looks like an over-specialised student rather than a better detector. The literature also almost never reports what adaptation does to *other* cameras, so measuring and repairing that cost is a genuine contribution. Detection-specific evidence that full fine-tuning distorts pretrained features comes from "Mind the Backbone". There, full fine-tuning of a ResNet-50 detector **more than halved out-of-domain Comic AP (15.7 → 7.5) while raising in-domain AP from 44.6 to 50.4**, and regularising toward pretrained weights plus SE blocks raised relative corruption robustness from 55.3% to 69.0% ([Saito et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.14744)). The same paper shows the effect depends on the backbone (ConvNeXt fine-tuning *improved* OOD), so YOLO26n's sensitivity must be measured, not assumed.

**WiSE-OD** is the strongest-supported fix. It extends WiSE-FT ([Wortsman et al., CVPR 2022](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/papers/Wortsman_Robust_Fine-Tuning_of_Zero-Shot_Models_CVPR_2022_paper.pdf)) from CLIP classifiers to Faster R-CNN, FCOS, RetinaNet and YOLOv8n. With Faster R-CNN, interpolating pretrained and fine-tuned weights at λ = 0.5 lifted in-domain LLVIP AP50 from 93.68 to 96.06 and corrupted-LLVIP mPC from 56.40 to 75.08, at zero inference cost ([WiSE-OD](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18925v2)). It is not universal: on real M3FD night images the merged model (43.3) lost to plain fine-tuning (47.8), and the authors note that gains shrink when either endpoint is weak ([WiSE-OD](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18925v2)). The precondition favours this paper, because the pretrained YOLO26n already scores 0.684 on the held-out camera. One YOLO-specific pitfall is BatchNorm: interpolated weights can cause activation "variance collapse". The standard remedy is to recompute running statistics by forwarding data after merging, and the REPAIR variant cut a ResNet-18 interpolation barrier from about 16% to 1.5% ([Git Re-Basin](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.04836); [variance collapse](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.12766)). Two endpoints from the same initialisation should suffer far less than independently trained nets, so the paper should report both raw and BN-recalibrated merges.

Freezing and replay are the two cheap complements. In WiSE-OD's detection benchmark, probing the head on a frozen backbone (LP, 70.96 corruption mPC) beat full fine-tuning (56.40) and matched LP-FT (70.02), which shows that **a frozen backbone alone recovers much of the robustness** ([WiSE-OD](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18925v2)). A non-peer-reviewed YOLOv8n study found that freezing the backbone kept COCO mAP within 0.1% of the original ([arXiv 2505.01016](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01016)). The paper should confirm its optimiser was set explicitly, because Ultralytics silently ignores `lr0` under the default `optimizer='auto'` ([Ultralytics train docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/train/)). L2-SP, which penalises distance from pretrained weights ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01483)), needs a custom trainer; WiSE is its cheaper post-hoc analogue. For replay, Scenes100 mixed **"the same number of images from MSCOCO training set as the number of unlabeled frames from the videos"** during per-scene pseudo-label adaptation ([Zhang & Hoai](https://www3.cs.stonybrook.edu/~minhhoai/papers/Self-Supervised_Scene_Adaptation_CVPR23.pdf)). Continual-detection work finds that replay buffers of 5–50% stay close to joint training ([arXiv 2602.13440](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13440v1)). COCO person images supply exactly what the student lost: small, distant people and many bench/bin/tree negatives.

The small-person collapse (32–64 px recall 0.40 → 0.05) has a plausible mechanism that the paper can test directly. Small objects receive lower teacher confidence and noisier boxes ([SCMT, IJCAI 2022](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2022/0207.pdf)), so a single 0.40 threshold censors them first. Unlabelled small people then become background supervision that teaches the student to suppress them. Raising pseudo-label recall trades off against precision ([ACRST](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.05031)), so the remedy is size-aware thresholds or masked "ignore" regions rather than a blanket threshold cut. No paper was found that studies per-size pseudo-label thresholds or this recall collapse in compact detectors, so a histogram of pseudo-label sizes against raw teacher outputs and held-out human labels would be a novel diagnostic.

**Recommended protocol.** Start with E0, which re-evaluates the existing pretrained and fine-tuned endpoints. E1 is a WiSE sweep over λ ∈ {0, 0.1, …, 1.0}, run raw and with BN recalibration on mixed campus and COCO images. The remaining arms are E2, 1:1 COCO-person replay with 10–20% person-free COCO negatives; E3, a backbone freeze with an explicit optimiser, 3–10× lower LR and 10–20 epochs; E4, size-aware pseudo-labels plus scale/multi-scale augmentation; and E5, WiSE applied to the best of E2–E4. Evaluate every arm on five things: the camera-1 test split, the held-out camera, a COCO-person subset for forgetting, size-stratified recall, and false positives per person-free frame. **Select λ and freeze depth on camera-1 validation plus COCO only, never on the held-out camera**, which must stay test-only. Plot camera-1 AP against held-out AP with the λ-path as a curve. Points above the straight pretrained-to-fine-tuned segment show effective robustness in the sense of Taori et al. ([Accuracy on the Line](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.04649)). Andreassen et al. warn that such robustness is rare and can vanish at convergence ([arXiv 2106.15831](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.15831)), so frame claims as Pareto improvements. For the reviewer's multi-scene request, use Scenes100's per-scene "average AP gain" protocol and its public videos ([code/data](https://github.com/cvlab-stonybrook/scenes100)) as extra OOD scenes, plus COCO-O for generic robustness ([COCO-O](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.12730)). Run two or three seeds for any difference under about 2 AP. Then feed out-of-domain AP into the decision matrix, which the reviewer correctly notes is currently robust to weights but not to shift.

## 182 correlated frames carry the evidence of perhaps 5–20 independent ones

The reviewer is right that an i.i.d. frame bootstrap understates uncertainty, and the arch documentation states it plainly: for persistent data "a time-series bootstrap with an appropriately chosen block size should be used" ([arch](https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/bootstrap/confidence-intervals.html)). Among the three standard schemes, the **circular block bootstrap (CBB) at fixed block length is the right primary choice**. After the Nordman/Patton–Politis–White correction, circular blocks have an asymptotic MSE ratio of (2/3)^(2/3) ≈ 0.763 relative to the stationary bootstrap. In finite-sample AR(1) simulations, CBB had lower MSE than the stationary bootstrap in every case ([Patton, Politis & White 2009](https://public.econ.duke.edu/~ap172/Patton_Politis_White_2009.pdf)). Lahiri showed that overlapping blocks beat non-overlapping ones ([Lahiri 1999](https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.aos/1018031117)). CBB also gives every frame equal weight by wrapping the series, and it reports results at the interpretable 2.5/5/10 s durations the reviewer asked for (L = 10/20/40 frames). The stationary bootstrap at the same mean lengths serves as a robustness row. All three schemes are available in Python's `arch.bootstrap`.

The arithmetic for n = 182 is sobering. Each replicate is rebuilt from **19, 10 or 5 blocks** at L = 10, 20 or 40, so the 10 s row rests on roughly five independent pieces. Theory puts the optimal block length at order n^{1/3} for variance estimation ([Hall, Horowitz & Jing](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/blocklength/html/hhj.html)), which is about 5.7 frames times a constant that grows with autocorrelation. The corrected Politis–White estimator in `arch.bootstrap.optimal_block_length` estimates that constant from sample autocorrelations ([arch docs](https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/bootstrap/generated/arch.bootstrap.optimal_block_length.html)). At n = 182, however, its maximum tuning lag is 19 frames, so it cannot see dependence beyond about 5 s, which is why the 10 s block is the conservative end of the range. Because AP is not a per-frame mean, the estimator needs a scalar proxy series. Jackknife pseudo-values of AP, or per-frame TP − FP counts, work. Under an AR(1) approximation, effective sample size is n(1−ρ)/(1+ρ) ([arXiv 2604.14414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14414)). Lag-1 correlations of 0.8, 0.9 and 0.95 then leave **about 20, 10 and 5 effective frames**. The paper should estimate ρ from its own data and report n_eff = n / (SE_block/SE_iid)² from the bootstrap itself.

For the AP computation, the established precedent is the PASCAL VOC bootstrap note and ILSVRC. Both resample test images, recompute the full metric on each replicate, and compare methods through the **paired difference** on the same replicate, because overlapping marginal intervals can hide a method that always wins ([VOC bootstrap note](https://homepages.inf.ed.ac.uk/ckiw/postscript/bootstrap_note.pdf); [ILSVRC](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.0575)). For video, the only change is that the resampling unit becomes a block. The efficient implementation runs COCO matching once per frame and caches scores, TP/FP flags per IoU threshold and GT counts. It then pools the cached records over each resampled index multiset, with the same indices for both models. pycocotools does not reliably weight duplicated image IDs, so this pooling step should be reimplemented. Use percentile intervals as primary, following VOC, ILSVRC and Bouthillier et al. ([arXiv 2103.03098](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03098)), with basic intervals as a check. Standard BCa estimates its acceleration from a leave-one-out jackknife over single observations ([arch](https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/bootstrap/confidence-intervals.html)), which ignores temporal dependence. For tracking, MOTA's ratio of sums can be block-resampled from per-block FN/FP/IDSW/GT sums. HOTA and IDF1 depend on a global identity assignment, so their intervals must be *conditional* on the full-sequence matching, and the paper should say so.

The "p < 0.001" claim needs a precise restatement. Under Phipson and Smyth, the exact Monte Carlo p-value is **(b + 1)/(B + 1)**. Naïve b/B p-values are "understated by about 1/m" and can return an invalid zero ([Phipson & Smyth](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.05766)). With B = 1,000 the minimum attainable value is 1/1001 ≈ 0.000999, so the correct text is "p = 1/(B+1), the minimum attainable (b = 0 of B = 1,000)". The test should also use a null-centred statistic, counting |Δ*_b − Δ̂| ≥ |Δ̂|, rather than the share of replicates below zero, and the rerun should use B = 9,999. A more rigorous complement is a **paired block sign-flip (randomisation) test** that swaps model outputs within whole blocks. Its exact minimum two-sided p is 2/2^K, which is 0.0625 with five 10 s blocks. That makes an honest point for the rebuttal: **no test of any kind can reach p < 0.05 on this clip at 10 s blocks**, and only more independent footage can fix that. McNemar and Wilcoxon tests assume independent pairs, so the paper should switch to clustered variants (`clust.bin.pair` for Obuchowski/Durkalski McNemar; `clusrank` for Rosner–Glynn–Lee Wilcoxon) with temporal blocks as clusters ([clust.bin.pair](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/clust.bin.pair/readme/README.html); [clusrank](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03409)). Block lengths are a sensitivity analysis rather than independent tests, so designate one primary L in advance and do not correct for multiplicity across L.

The paper should define its units explicitly. Dror et al. call dependence between test items an open problem that "violates the conditions under which the theoretical guarantees" of standard tests hold ([Dror et al., ACL 2018](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)). The proposed definitions are: the **frame** is the observation unit; the **temporal block** is the resampling unit; the **track** is the identity unit; and the **scene/video** is the generalisation unit. With one test video, every interval describes within-scene sampling variability only. The held-out camera should appear as a separate scene-level replicate rather than being pooled into the frame bootstrap. With several scenes, a hierarchical bootstrap that resamples scenes first keeps type-I error within bounds ([Saravanan et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906290)). Below roughly 5–10 scenes, per-scene tables are more honest than pooled intervals. The recommended table has rows L ∈ {1 (i.i.d.), Politis–White b̂, 10, 20, 40}. Its columns are point estimate, 95% CI, half-width, inflation factor relative to i.i.d., n_eff and paired p-value. If the half-width is still rising at L = 40, the paper should state that dependence extends past 10 s.

## The 2B model proposes; a deterministic policy layer disposes

Agent-security research converges on one principle: models cannot be trusted to resist injection, so the architecture must make it "impossible for that input to trigger any consequential actions" ([Design Patterns for Securing LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08837v3)). The paper's two-stage redesign already instantiates two of the six named patterns, and it should say so. Text-only tool routing is an **Action-Selector**, and a tool-less vision stage is the quarantined half of a **Dual-LLM** split ([Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2023/Apr/25/dual-llm-pattern/)). CaMeL shows what full provenance tracking costs: it solves 77% of AgentDojo tasks with provable security against 84% undefended ([CaMeL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18813)). AgentDojo's own defence comparison shows that architectural restriction beats detection. A **tool filter cut GPT-4o's targeted attack success rate (ASR) to 6.84% while keeping 73.13% benign utility**. A DeBERTa prompt-injection detector reached 7.95% ASR but collapsed benign utility to 41.49%, and data delimiting barely helped (41.65% ASR) ([AgentDojo](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13352v3)). Spotlighting reported >50% → <2% ASR on GPT models in its own paper ([Hines et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14720)), but that gap with AgentDojo's result argues for treating it only as defence in depth.

The policy layer the reviewer requests maps onto OWASP LLM06:2025 "Excessive Agency". Its mitigations include minimal permissions, human approval for high-impact actions, rate limiting and **complete mediation**, meaning authorisation is enforced downstream "rather than letting the LLM decide" ([OWASP LLM06](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm062025-excessive-agency/)). Concretely, the layer has several parts. The operator's role comes from an authenticated session and is never parsed from chat text. A per-role RBAC tool filter applies: viewers query, operators set lines and alerts, and supervisors remove alerts or lower thresholds below a floor. Server-side schema and range validation rejects out-of-frame lines, NaN, negative values, overflow and unknown zone IDs, and never silently "repairs" them. High-impact changes require UI confirmation bound to a hash of the exact arguments. A rate limit applies, alert removal has a cooldown, and the layer fails closed. Confirmation should be limited to high-impact actions because the design-patterns paper warns of approval fatigue ([Design Patterns](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08837v3)). Every decision goes to a **hash-chained audit log**, in which each entry stores H(prev ∥ entry). This follows Schneier–Kelsey forward-secure logs ([Schneier & Kelsey](https://www.schneier.com/academic/archives/1999/05/secure_audit_logs_to.html)), with periodically signed checkpoints exported off-device in the spirit of Certificate Transparency ([RFC 6962](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6962)). The log should be evaluated with synthetic modify, delete, reorder and truncate attacks, and every one must be detected. Truncation after the last checkpoint is detectable only with external anchoring.

Visual prompt injection is the threat most specific to this system, since signs, placards and T-shirts in the camera's view can carry text. The closest published measurement covers Qwen3-VL-4B. **Typographic-image attack success ran 40.8–48.2% at 8–20 px fonts, nearly matching its 48.9% plain-text rate.** Rotation by 90° (18.2%) and heavy blur (25.2%) reduced it, whereas GPT-4o fell from 35.6% text to 7.7% image ([Cisco, "Reading Between the Pixels"](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.12371)). FigStep's typographic jailbreak averaged 82.5% across six open LVLMs ([FigStep](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.05608)). No published numbers exist for any VLM under 3B parameters or for CCTV-style scenes, so the paper must measure them, and a surveillance-scene injection set would itself be new. Because stage 2 has no tools, the realistic harms are answer manipulation (a false count), cross-stage leakage (a stage-2 answer re-entering stage-1 history as a command) and operator deception. Each needs its own test cases. The paper's 0.00 specificity on fabricated alerts deserves emphasis in this context. It is an instance of the risk-awareness failure that R-Judge benchmarks, where even the best model judged agent records only 74.42% correctly ([R-Judge](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2401.10019)). That supports keeping alerts deterministic.

**Recommended protocol.** Build roughly 300 hand-authored base cases. About 60–100 are authorized commands; the rest are unsafe cases across ambiguous, conflicting, unauthorized, malformed-argument, alert-disabling, direct-injection, frame-injection, tool-result-injection, multi-turn escalation and flooding categories, at 20–50 each. Frame injections are rendered onto held-out frames across font size, contrast, rotation and blur. Report AgentDojo's three metrics ([AgentDojo](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13352v3)): authorized success rate (benign utility), utility under attack, and unsafe-request rejection rate (1 − targeted ASR). Add a false-rejection rate so that refusing everything cannot score perfectly. Add τ-bench's **pass^k** over k seeded or paraphrased runs to expose stochastic routing failures ([τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045)), and attribute each block to the layer that made it. The key ablation has three rows: model alone, model plus intent-check fallback, and the full policy layer. Scoring should use deterministic state diffs and the audit log rather than an LLM judge, which is a strength worth stating. Use Wilson intervals throughout ([Brown, Cai & DasGupta](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/statistical-science/volume-16/issue-2/Interval-Estimation-for-a-Binomial-Proportion/10.1214/ss/1009213286.full)). Zero failures in 50 cases bounds the true rate below 7.1%, and zero in 100 bounds it below 3.7%, so **at least 50 cases per headline category** are needed. Write the attack strings before tuning the guardrails, or hold out a test split. Add about 20 adaptive human red-team cases reported separately, and release the JSONL suite and scorer. Credible published suites range from ToolEmu's 144 cases to InjecAgent's 1,054 ([ToolEmu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15817); [InjecAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02691)), so 300 sits comfortably within precedent.

## Published Orin numbers predict headroom, but nobody has measured concurrency

The reference numbers are favourable, with one caveat. Ultralytics reports YOLO26 nano TensorRT FP16 model-only latency at 640 px of **4.57 ms on Orin Nano Super, 4.13 ms on Orin NX 16GB and 2.62 ms on AGX Orin 64GB**. INT8 saves only about 17% further and costs about 3 mAP points on Nano and NX. These figures exclude pre- and post-processing, and the guide does not state JetPack or power mode ([Ultralytics Jetson guide](https://docs.ultralytics.com/guides/nvidia-jetson/)). Community reports of 7–13 ms per inference for YOLO11n on Orin Nano probably include pipeline overhead ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/expected-fps-for-yolox-s-tensorrt-engine-on-jetson-orin-nano-jetpack-6-2/329875)). No published Orin numbers exist above 640 px, which matters because the paper deploys at 736×1280. For the VLM, NVIDIA's TensorRT-Edge-LLM benchmarks Qwen3-VL-2B with an INT4 AWQ LLM and FP16 ViT at batch 1.

| Orin Nano 8GB | 151.1 | 190.9 | 36.9 | 4,380 |

A 32-token answer on Orin Nano therefore costs roughly 1.1–1.3 s in isolation. The paper's llama.cpp GGUF path is likely slower, and no controlled llama.cpp Qwen-VL numbers on Orin Nano were found. Output length dominates energy. An edge-VLM study on Orin NX found each output token costs 11–39× the time of an input token, and capping output length saved up to 97% of energy, versus about 10% from pruning visual tokens ([Zhan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09520)). The paper should therefore fix `n_predict` and report it.

Memory and contention are where real risk lies. Several Orin Nano reports describe **NvMap allocation failures (error 12)**. NVIDIA staff tied these to an allocation limit introduced by a security patch in L4T r36.4.7, and separately to fragmentation ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/orin-nano-qwen3-vl-4b/348122)). Another report shows PyTorch and llama.cpp sharing a CUDA context on an Orin Nano 8GB, failing with an NVML assertion and an out-of-memory error on a 929 MB buffer ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/pytorch-cudacachingallocator-nvml-assertion-when-sharing-cuda-context-with-llama-cpp-on-orin-nano-8-gb-jetpack-6-2-2/370049)). The deployment should therefore run the detector and VLM as separate processes with no PyTorch in the detector path, log tegrastats' largest-free-block field and report the exact L4T revision. CUDA MPS has been nominally supported since JetPack 6.1, but its server does not auto-launch on Orin NX and static partitioning fails on Orin Nano ([MPS forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/proper-use-of-cuda-mps/337315); [partitioning forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/static-partitioning-failed-with-nvidia-mps-on-jetson-orin-nano/363234)). Treat MPS as an optional arm, not a requirement. No published measurement exists of detector tail-latency inflation while a VLM shares an Orin iGPU, so this paper's measurement would be the first.

**Recommended protocol.** Borrow MLPerf Edge's discipline: runs of at least 600 s, p90 latency for single-stream and p99 for multi-stream, and the same code for accuracy and performance ([MLCommons rules](https://github.com/mlcommons/inference_policies/blob/master/inference_rules.adoc)). Record the module, JetPack/L4T, TensorRT and llama.cpp versions, and the nvpmodel mode **by name and ID**. Mode ID 0 means 15 W on Orin Nano but MaxN on NX and AGX ([NVIDIA r36.4 power docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/jetson/archives/r36.4/DeveloperGuide/SD/PlatformPowerAndPerformance/JetsonOrinNanoSeriesJetsonOrinNxSeriesAndJetsonAgxOrinSeries.html)). Also record jetson_clocks state, fan profile, ambient temperature and storage. Run four configurations in one power mode: detector + ByteTrack alone, VLM alone, both concurrently at the real trigger rate, and the concurrent setup with priority controls (nice/taskset, a lower trigger rate, an output cap, optionally MPS). Feed a fixed-rate real-time source with NVDEC hardware decoding, discard warm-up, and report cold-start time separately. Each configuration runs for 30–60 minutes, logging tegrastats at 100–1000 ms. Report end-to-end p50/p95/p99/max latency, isolated engine latency via trtexec, frame-drop rate against a 33.3 ms deadline, and VLM encode/prefill/decode times under load. Also report peak RAM and swap, and compare the first and last five minutes to expose throttling. Flag any event at the documented thresholds: 99 °C software throttling, or the Orin Nano 8GB over-current limit of 15 W average VDD_IN ([NVIDIA r36.4 power docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/jetson/archives/r36.4/DeveloperGuide/SD/PlatformPowerAndPerformance/JetsonOrinNanoSeriesJetsonOrinNxSeriesAndJetsonAgxOrinSeries.html)). Compute J/frame as integrated VDD_IN over windows of at least 60 s divided by frames processed. Do not sum the sub-rails, because VDD_IN is already total module power, and community reports say the INA3221 is too slow for per-frame attribution ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/externally-measuring-current-draw-power/372739)). Repeat everything at least three times. Add the reviewer's operational stressors — RTSP disconnect and recovery, bursts of VLM requests, sustained SQLite writes — as scripted fault-injection runs. No literature sets their protocol, so a clear description of the procedure is enough. Orin Nano 8GB is the most defensible primary target because it is the tightest memory budget.

## Two blind annotators set the noise ceiling for every headline metric

The annotation rules can be adopted wholesale from the benchmarks reviewers already trust. MOT16 annotates the **full-extent box**, estimating occluded parts from "expected size, shadows, reflections, previous and future frames". It assigns ambiguous classes (static seated people, reflections, mannequins, posters) that are neither rewarded nor penalised. It keeps an ID only "as long as the person is inside the field of view and its path can be determined unambiguously" and issues a new ID on re-entry. It ran a detector only *after* manual labelling, as a completeness check ([MOT16](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.00831)). Caltech's ignore semantics are precise: ignored ground truth "need not be matched, however, matches are not considered mistakes either", and boxes under 20 px, truncated boxes and unseparable groups are always ignored ([Dollár et al., PAMI 2012](https://pdollar.github.io/files/papers/DollarPAMI12peds.pdf)). CityPersons defines the standard "reasonable" stratum as height ≥ 50 px with ≤ 35% occlusion ([CityPersons](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.05693)). CrowdHuman adds visible and head boxes and double-checks every annotation with a second annotator ([CrowdHuman](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.00123)). The protocol must state whether full or visible boxes are scored. If the teacher ensemble effectively predicts visible extents while humans draw full extents, occluded people will be systematically penalised.

For agreement, the reference points are concrete. Two independent PASCAL VOC expert box sets agree at **88% mean IoU**, which equals a 4 px perturbation of every coordinate ([Papadopoulos et al., ICCV 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02750)). A TAO tracking re-annotation reached 0.93 mean IoU, though that figure was seen only in the supplement snippet ([TAO supplement](https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2020/papers_ECCV/papers/123500426-supp.pdf)). Krippendorff's α has been extended to bounding boxes, with α ≥ 0.667 acceptable and ≥ 0.8 good. Reported values range from 0.881 on VIRAT to 0.575 on Stanford Drone, and person-class α spans 0.463–0.817 across annotator groups ([Nassar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.10107)); the `iaa-od` package implements it ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/iaa-od/)). For tracks, HOTA's Jaccard-based formulation is roughly symmetric whereas MOTA is not ([Luiten et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.07736)), so **annotator-B-versus-annotator-A HOTA, AssA and IDF1** form a natural human ceiling. No published human-versus-human HOTA for pedestrian MOT was found, which makes this a small novel contribution. Agreement degrades with size, since IoU between equally plausible boxes correlates with the smallest box dimension at Spearman ρ = 0.79 ([Murrugarra-Llerena et al., CVPRW 2022](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022W/VDU/papers/Murrugarra-Llerena_Can_We_Trust_Bounding_Box_Annotations_for_Object_Detection_CVPRW_2022_paper.pdf)). Small-pedestrian results should therefore use IoU 0.5 as primary.

Independence from the teacher is the crux of the reviewer's objection, and pre-annotation threatens it. In treebank construction, editing parser output produced "a clear anchoring effect" that **overestimated parser performance** ([Berzak et al., EMNLP 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.04481)). No equivalent controlled study exists for bounding boxes, but the risk transfers directly. The evaluation subset should therefore be annotated from scratch with no teacher boxes visible. If cost forces pre-annotation, proposals should come from a model outside the teacher ensemble, and a randomly chosen 10–20% of frames should be labelled from scratch to quantify any anchoring shift. Absolute AP is fragile to the reference: swapping VOC's annotated boxes for mask-derived ones dropped EfficientDet-D0 and SSD300 by **7.6 and 9.3 points AP50:95**, and sub-pixel discrepancies reordered COCO small-object rankings ([Murrugarra-Llerena et al.](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022W/VDU/papers/Murrugarra-Llerena_Can_We_Trust_Bounding_Box_Annotations_for_Object_Detection_CVPRW_2022_paper.pdf)). A 2026 re-annotation found up to 40% more objects in COCO, mostly small, occluded or dense, yet "model rankings remain largely stable" (abstract only) ([Penquitt et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21822)). The revision should therefore report **rank agreement (Kendall τ) between pseudo-label and human ground truth** alongside absolute metrics. If the rankings agree, the paper's relative conclusions survive even where absolute numbers move.

Counting needs event-level evaluation, because totals hide offsetting errors. An NCHRP comparison warns that "undercounting and overcounting could be occurring that cancel each other out" ([NCHRP](https://www.nap.edu/read/24732/chapter/5)), which is exactly the paper's own fragmentation-plus-missed-detection example. The AI City Challenge compares cumulative counts at segment boundaries ([AI City 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14619)). A TU/e pedestrian benchmark triggers crossings only when most position pairs one second apart fall on opposite sides of the line, because the naive two-point check is noise-sensitive ([arXiv 2108.11719](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.11719)). No standard temporal tolerance for matching crossing events exists, so the paper must define one. **Recommended protocol.** Two annotators label, blind and from scratch in CVAT track mode with keyframe interpolation ([CVAT](https://docs.cvat.ai/docs/annotation/manual-annotation/modes/track-mode-basics/)), using MOT16 ID rules and Caltech ignore semantics. Coverage is several multi-minute clips spanning both cameras, plus sparsely sampled detection frames (one every 2–5 s) chosen to fill size, occlusion, density, illumination and viewpoint quotas. A third person adjudicates only unmatched boxes and pairs below IoU 0.5–0.7, and agreement is reported *before* adjudication. The paper should then report matched-F1, mean IoU and Krippendorff's α between annotators; teacher pseudo-labels scored against human ground truth per stratum; every student metric under both ground truths, with Kendall τ; line-crossing precision, recall and F1 from one-to-one, direction-matched events at τ ∈ {0.5, 1, 2} s; signed and absolute count error per 30–60 s bin; occupancy MAE against human counts; and near-field and far-field results separately. The author-run AI-assistant audit should be replaced by, or at least validated against, this human audit. Budgets must be realistic: Caltech needed about 400 annotator-hours for 2.3 h of video ([Dollár et al.](https://pdollar.github.io/files/papers/DollarPAMI12peds.pdf)). Binomial arithmetic puts about 300–500 roughly independent boxes per stratum at ±3–4 percentage points of recall (my planning estimate, not a sourced standard). Strata should therefore be reported marginally, not as a full cross.

## RF-DETR Nano's native 384 px means the current pairing is neither pixel- nor compute-matched

The reviewer's resolution concern is sharper than the manuscript acknowledges. RF-DETR's Pareto-optimal Nano configuration runs at **384 px** with patch size 16 and two windows, not 640. It scores 48.4 AP on COCO at 2.3 ms on a T4 with 31.9 GFLOPs. Valid resolutions are multiples of 32, and the NAS search covered only 320–960 px ([RF-DETR, ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09554); [RF-DETR FAQ](https://rfdetr.roboflow.com/latest/faq/)). YOLO26n scores 40.9 AP at 640 with 5.5 GFLOPs and 1.7 ms on a T4 ([Ultralytics YOLO26](https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/yolo26/)). Because FLOPs scale roughly with pixel count, YOLO26n at 1280 costs about 22 GFLOPs while RF-DETR-N at 640 costs at least about 89 (my extrapolations). The paper's pairing therefore gives YOLO about **four times the pixels** while giving RF-DETR about **four times the compute**. The large small-object AP gap (0.552 vs 0.298) is more plausibly a pixel effect than an architecture effect. RF-DETR's own authors state that "higher resolution improves small object detection performance". At native settings, RF-DETR-N actually beats YOLOv11-N on COCO small-object AP (25.2 vs 17.3) despite using fewer input pixels ([RF-DETR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09554)). The only official nano-at-1280 datapoint, YOLOv5n6, gains 8 AP over YOLOv5n at 640, though it also adds a P6 output ([YOLOv5](https://github.com/ultralytics/yolov5)). The paper's reported GFLOPs (13.6 and 97.3) should be regenerated with one profiler at the exact input shapes evaluated.

Measurement hygiene matters as much as the sweep. The RF-DETR paper found that vendor-reported YOLO accuracy often fails to reproduce under TensorRT FP16. Its reported YOLOv11-M figures of 51.5 AP / 4.7 ms became 48.3 AP / 5.2 ms under buffered FP16, and a naïve D-FINE export collapsed to 0.5 AP. It therefore advocates using the same artifact for accuracy and latency, including NMS for YOLO, and inserting a 200 ms buffer between passes to avoid throttling ([RF-DETR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09554)). Even Roboflow's own table lists YOLO26-N at 40.3 AP rather than Ultralytics' 40.9 ([RF-DETR benchmarks](https://rfdetr.roboflow.com/latest/learn/benchmarks/)), so every number in the revision must come from one in-house harness. RT-DETR, D-FINE and DEIM settle the comparison by fixing 640×640 for all models ([RT-DETR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08069)). RF-DETR instead compares on latency-matched Pareto curves. Both conventions are legitimate, and the paper can report both.

**Recommended protocol.** Fine-tune both students at each of {384, 512, 640, 960, 1280}, or at minimum at {640, 1280} for both plus RF-DETR-N at its native 384. Flag that RF-DETR at 1280 lies outside its trained positional-embedding range. Add train-at-A/test-at-B cells so deployment-time resizing is visible. At every point report AP50, AP50:95, per-height-bin recall (<32, 32–64, 64–128, >128 px) on human ground truth, GFLOPs from one profiler, and measured Orin TensorRT FP16 latency, memory and J/frame. Plot AP against latency and against GFLOPs, and compare at matched latency, for example the best YOLO resolution whose latency does not exceed RF-DETR-N@384. A third arm of SAHI-tiled RF-DETR-N separates "more pixels" from "different model". SAHI added 5.1–6.8 AP at inference on aerial small-object data ([SAHI, ICIP 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06934)), though no surveillance-pedestrian SAHI numbers exist. Retitle the existing comparison as a "deployment-configuration" result and present the sweep as the controlled architecture result.

## Conclusion

The most useful shift the literature offers is to treat several of the reviewer's "gaps" as **unclaimed contributions** rather than defects to patch. Scene-adaptation papers report in-scene gains and almost never measure damage to other cameras. No published work quantifies small-object recall collapse under pseudo-label fine-tuning, human-versus-human HOTA for pedestrian tracking, visual prompt injection for VLMs under 3B parameters or in CCTV scenes, or detector tail latency when a VLM shares an Orin iGPU. Each experiment above therefore both answers the review and fills a documented hole. Together they support the reviewer's suggested reframing toward reliable closed-loop analytics under pseudo-label uncertainty, scene shift and tool-use failure.

The second lesson is structural. The same principle runs through every section: never let an unverified component be the sole authority. Deterministic analytics own counts and alerts; a policy engine, not the 2B model, owns state changes; human ground truth, not the teacher, owns accuracy claims; and the scene, not the frame, owns generalisation claims. The statistics make the last point unavoidable. With five 10 s blocks no test can reach p < 0.05, so more frames from the same video cannot rescue the generalisation argument. Only more independent scenes can. That makes multi-scene evaluation with WiSE-style mitigation the revision's centre of gravity, ahead of tighter confidence intervals on the original clip.
