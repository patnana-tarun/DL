\begin{abstract}
A common way to adapt a crowd-analytics system to a new camera is to pseudo-label its footage with large teacher
detectors and fine-tune a compact student on the result. Prior work reports the gain on the adapted camera. We
measure what the adaptation costs everywhere else. Fine-tuning \yolo{} on pseudo-labels from one plaza video raises
its \AP{} there from \MsPtAa{} to \MsFtAa{}, but lowers it on all three other cameras we tested and on human-labelled
COCO people; on a test-only video it drops from \MsPtHo{} to \MsFtHo{}, well below its own pretrained weights. Part
of the loss comes from the pseudo-label score threshold, which discards \LtAaA{} of the teacher boxes on people
shorter than 32\,px and so trains the student to treat distant people as background. The rest is forgetting.
Averaging the pretrained and fine-tuned weights, with the mixing coefficient chosen on other cameras' validation
data, keeps most of the in-domain gain (\MsSelAa) and restores the test-only score (\MsSelHo) at no training or
inference cost, whereas re-estimating BatchNorm statistics after averaging makes it worse. At equal input size,
\rfdetr{} matches \yolo{} on the adapted camera and transfers far better, so the CNN's in-domain lead came from its
larger input. The selected detector feeds a streaming runtime in which Qwen3-VL-2B answers operator questions and
edits alert rules through tool calls. The model repeats whatever count it is given, confirms fabricated alerts, and
follows an instruction painted on a sign in the camera view once that text enters the chat history. A deterministic
policy layer outside the model raises the rejection of unsafe requests on a 300-case suite from \SfGrTestRej{} to
\SfPoTestRej{} with no loss in task success. All timings were measured on a desktop GPU.
\end{abstract}
