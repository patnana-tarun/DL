\section{Introduction}
Manual annotation of footage for every new camera is costly, so crowd-analytics systems are frequently adapted
without human labels. Large teacher detectors label video from the target camera, and a compact student suited to
the deployment hardware is fine-tuned on the resulting
pseudo-labels~\cite{radosavovic2018datadistill,xie2020noisystudent,zhang2023scenes100}. Scenes100 showed that this
procedure improves detection on each of 100 fixed cameras~\cite{zhang2023scenes100}. That study, like most work on
scene adaptation, evaluates the student only on the camera to which it was adapted. In practice, the same weights are
often deployed on a second camera, or the original camera is repositioned, without any re-labelling. The extent to
which an adapted compact real-time detector degrades on such cameras, and the causes of that degradation, have not
been quantified.

A second development in recent systems is a vision-language model (VLM) that answers operator questions and may
change the system configuration through tool calls. Prompt injection against tool-using agents has been studied for
web, e-mail and document workflows~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhan2024injecagent}, and typographic images are known
to jailbreak open VLMs~\cite{gong2025figstep}. A camera, however, introduces an input channel that has not been
examined in this setting: any person in the field of view can present written text to the model.

This paper presents a complete pipeline of this kind and evaluates each component on scenes it was not designed for.
Two teachers (RF-DETR Large and YOLO26x) pseudo-label a 5-minute plaza video; a CNN student (\yolo{}) and a
transformer student (\rfdetr{}) are fine-tuned on it; and the selected student operates in a streaming runtime with
ByteTrack, rule-based analytics and a Qwen3-VL-2B operator agent. Evaluation covers the adaptation video, three
further campus videos, one of which is reserved exclusively for testing, and human-labelled COCO person images. The
main contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A quantification of the cross-camera cost of single-camera pseudo-label fine-tuning. Fine-tuning \yolo{} raises
\AP{} on the adapted camera and lowers it on every other camera and on COCO people, in each case below the
pretrained weights. The same holds when either of two other scenes is used as the adaptation scene, whereas \rfdetr{},
fine-tuned identically, improves on the other cameras and loses little on COCO.
\item An identification of the size-blind pseudo-label threshold as one cause. The threshold removes most teacher
boxes on small people, so the student learns to classify them as background. Marking these boxes as ignore regions
recovers part of the held-out loss but none of the COCO forgetting, which separates the two causes.
\item A label-free mitigation. Averaging pretrained and fine-tuned weights~\cite{wortsman2022wise}, with the mixing
coefficient selected on validation data from other cameras, recovers the cross-camera accuracy. For this
BatchNorm-based detector, interpolating the normalisation statistics outperforms re-estimating them, contrary to the
usual recommendation for merged networks.
\item A resolution-controlled comparison showing that the in-domain advantage of the CNN student over the
transformer vanishes at equal input size; the CNN's benefit is a 24--44$\times$ reduction in compute at the same
resolution.
\item An evaluation of camera-borne prompt injection against a VLM operator agent. On a 300-case adversarial suite,
the 2B agent executes more than half of the unsafe requests on its own, and text painted in the camera view reaches
its tools through the chat history. A policy layer that keeps all authority outside the model, and isolates the
tool-calling stage from model-generated text, blocks these cases.
\end{enumerate}
All confidence intervals are circular-block bootstrap intervals over contiguous segments of video, because treating
frames as independent underestimates the standard error by a factor of about three in these data. The runtime
targets an NVIDIA Jetson Orin~\cite{jetsonorin}, but all timings reported here were measured on a desktop GPU. The
code, result files, evaluation scripts and a blind human-annotation protocol are publicly released.
