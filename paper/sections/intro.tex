\section{Introduction}
Labelling footage by hand for every new camera is expensive, so crowd-analytics systems are often adapted without
human labels. Large teacher detectors label video from the target camera, and a compact student that fits the
deployment hardware is fine-tuned on those
pseudo-labels~\cite{radosavovic2018datadistill,xie2020noisystudent,zhang2023scenes100}. Scenes100 showed that this
improves detection on each of 100 fixed cameras~\cite{zhang2023scenes100}. That study, like most work on scene
adaptation, scores the student on the camera it was adapted to. In practice the same weights end up on a second
camera, or the first camera is moved, and nobody re-labels anything. How much the adapted student loses on those
cameras, and why, has not been measured for compact real-time detectors.

A second change in recent systems is a vision-language model (VLM) that answers operator questions and can change the
system's configuration through tool calls. Prompt injection against tool-using agents has been studied for web,
e-mail and document workflows~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhan2024injecagent}, and typographic images are known to
jailbreak open VLMs~\cite{gong2025figstep}. A camera adds an input channel that has not been examined in this
setting: anyone who walks into view with a written sign can put text in front of the model.

We built a complete pipeline of this kind and tested each part on scenes it was not built for. Two teachers
(RF-DETR Large and YOLO26x) pseudo-label a 5-minute plaza video; a CNN student (\yolo{}) and a transformer student
(\rfdetr{}) are fine-tuned on it; the selected student runs in a streaming runtime with ByteTrack, rule-based
analytics and a Qwen3-VL-2B operator agent. We evaluate on the adaptation video, on three other campus videos, one of
which is never used for any decision, and on human-labelled COCO people. The findings that are new are these:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Single-camera pseudo-label fine-tuning of \yolo{} raises \AP{} on the adapted camera and lowers it on every
other camera and on COCO people, each time to below the pretrained weights. The same holds when either of two other
scenes is the adaptation scene. \rfdetr{}, fine-tuned the same way, improves on the other cameras and loses little on COCO.
\item A size-blind pseudo-label threshold is one cause. It removes most teacher boxes on small people, so the student
learns to label them background. Marking those boxes as ignore regions recovers part of the held-out loss but none of
the COCO forgetting, which separates the two causes.
\item Averaging pretrained and fine-tuned weights~\cite{wortsman2022wise} repairs the damage without any label from the
target camera, provided the mixing coefficient is chosen on validation data from other cameras. For this
BatchNorm detector, interpolating the normalisation statistics works better than re-estimating them, against the usual
advice for merged networks.
\item The CNN student's in-domain lead over the transformer disappears at equal input size. What the CNN offers is
24--44$\times$ less compute at the same resolution.
\item On a 300-case adversarial suite, the 2B VLM agent carries out more than half of the unsafe requests on its own, and text painted
in the camera view reaches its tools through the chat history. A policy layer that keeps all authority outside the
model, and keeps the tool-calling stage away from model-written text, stops these cases.
\end{enumerate}
All intervals are circular-block bootstrap intervals over contiguous stretches of video, since treating frames as
independent understates the standard error by a factor of about three here. The runtime is designed for an NVIDIA
Jetson Orin~\cite{jetsonorin}, but every timing in this paper was measured on a desktop GPU. Code, result files,
the evaluation scripts and a blind human-annotation protocol are released.
